Designing the VLANs

A VLAN is really just a way of running multiple separate networks over the same physical wiring and the same WiFi hardware. Instead of buying four routers and four sets of cables for four separate networks, you tag traffic with a network ID and let one router keep the networks logically apart while deciding what, if anything, is allowed to cross between them. That's the whole trick behind it. Creating the VLANs themselves is mechanical and fast. Deciding what should be allowed to talk to what is the actual design problem, and that's a story for the next page.

The four networks I landed on

I settled on four VLANs, managed through the TP-Link Omada controller, which I run locally on a Windows machine with cloud management turned off. I didn't want to depend on, or pay for, a cloud service just to manage a router sitting five feet away from me.

VLANNamePurpose
1Trusted wiredA couple of wired PCs that stayed on the original default network
10MainPhones, laptops, the gaming PC's WiFi, general trusted devices
20GuestVisitor devices, internet access only
30IoTSmart plugs, cameras, casting devices, smart TVs

VLAN 1 exists mostly because a couple of wired PCs on the network couldn't easily be moved onto a different VLAN without extra switch configuration I didn't want to deal with at the time, so I let them stay on the original default network instead of forcing a migration for no real benefit. That's a small compromise, but it's an honest one. Not every device needs to move onto a shiny new network just because it would make the diagram look cleaner.

Why Guest and IoT are separate networks

It would have been simpler to lump "everything that isn't a trusted device" into one leftover network. I split Guest and IoT apart on purpose instead, because they have genuinely different trust levels and genuinely different needs. A guest's phone should get internet and nothing else, full stop. There's no reason it should ever be able to see my smart plugs or reach anything on my main network. IoT devices need more nuance than that. I actually want to control them from my phone, cast video to a Chromecast, or stream from a smart TV that's sitting on the IoT network. Those are real, wanted interactions that a blanket "IoT gets internet only" policy would break outright.

The tradeoff I went back and forth on

One design choice I kept second guessing was whether Main WiFi, VLAN 10, should be treated the same as the wired trusted network, VLAN 1, or whether it should be more restricted, maybe only allowed to reach the IoT network and nothing else. I eventually decided that my phone and laptop on WiFi should have the same level of trust as a PC plugged straight into the router with a cable. If I trust a device enough to let it connect to my network at all, whether it's wired or wireless shouldn't be the thing that decides how much it's allowed to do. So the final policy treats VLAN 1 and VLAN 10 as equally trusted, both able to reach Guest and IoT, with Guest and IoT locked out of reaching back into either of them. That's the shape of the whole design. It's not maximally locked down, and it's not maximally simple either. It's built around the actual devices I own and what I actually want them to be able to do, which felt like a better test to design against than "what would a security textbook recommend for a network it's never seen."

VLAN trust map

      VLAN 1 (Trusted wired)  ----\
                                    >---- can reach ----> VLAN 20 (Guest)
      VLAN 10 (Main WiFi)     ----/                  \--> VLAN 30 (IoT)

      VLAN 20 (Guest)  ---- internet only, no access back to 1, 10, or 30

      VLAN 30 (IoT)    ---- internet only by default, cannot initiate
                            connections back to VLAN 1 or VLAN 10

A small management headache: a wired PC that wasn't wrong, just invisible

While I was in and out of the Omada controller getting all of this set up, one wired PC that was plugged directly into the router, and had full internet access the entire time, never showed up in the controller's client list. That's a strange kind of bug to run into, because the part that actually matters, the PC being online and working, was completely fine the whole time. It just wasn't visible in the management interface, which made me second guess whether the connection was really as solid as it looked, even though nothing was actually wrong with it.

It turned out to be a stale entry in the controller rather than an actual networking problem, and it cleared up once I renewed the DHCP lease and let the controller pick the device back up on its own. The lesson that stuck with me here is one I ran into more than once during this project. A device working correctly and a device showing up correctly in your management tools are two separate things, and it's worth checking both before assuming something is actually broken.
