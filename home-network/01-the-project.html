The Project

This started with a boring problem. My office had bad WiFi. That's really it. I wasn't trying to build a homelab, I wasn't chasing a hobby, I just wanted to sit at my desk without my connection dropping every time I moved my chair. If the WiFi in that one room had just worked, none of the rest of this probably happens.

This is a new apartment, and the builders had run Ethernet wiring into the walls but never actually finished it. I opened up the little junction box in my closet and found a bundle of blue cables just sitting there, unconnected to anything. I figured those were probably meant for internet, since blue is the usual color for network cable, but there was no jack, no connector, nothing to actually plug into. I asked the apartment about it and they said wiring inside the unit wasn't their responsibility and to try to call AT&T next who would obviously be no help. Hiring an electrician just to crimp a few connectors felt like a lot of money for something that looked pretty learnable, so I bought a cheap crimping tool, watched a few videos on wire order, and decided this was as good a reason as any to just learn how to do it myself. It worked on the first real test, which surprised me more than it probably should have. Plugging a laptop straight into the wall jack in my office and getting a real wired connection was the moment this stopped being a one-off fix and started being a project with actual momentum.

How the question changed

Once that wall jack was live, the question in my head shifted. It went from "how do I fix my WiFi" to "what does a wired access point in that room actually need behind it." That's a small shift on paper, but it's the one that turned this into a networking project instead of a weekend fix. Answering it meant learning what a router actually does versus what an access point does, which I honestly hadn't thought about clearly before this. I started looking at routers built for that kind of setup and ran into the idea of VLANs for the first time, without really understanding what they were yet.

At the same time, I had a growing pile of smart home devices sitting on the same network as everything else I owned. A couple of Google Nest devices, some Meross smart plugs, a Eufy camera. Nothing about that setup was broken exactly, but something in the back of my head kept saying those devices probably shouldn't have the same level of access to my network as my laptop does. That instinct is what eventually pulled VLANs from "a term I saw in a forum post" into "a thing I actually need to understand and build."

Where self-hosting came in

Around the same time, I got interested in running a few small services myself instead of relying on other people's apps and cloud accounts for everything. Pi-hole for network-wide ad blocking, a small server I could stream games from to the living room TV, maybe something for the smart home stuff down the line. None of it was urgent and none of it solved a problem I was actively suffering from. It was cheap to try, though, and it gave me a real reason to learn how a network actually works instead of just accepting whatever the ISP's box does out of the box.

So the project grew in layers, and that's basically how the rest of these pages are organized too. First the hardware, because none of the rest of this works without picking the right boxes and getting them wired up correctly. Then the home server, because I wanted one small machine handling everything self-hosted instead of buying three separate devices for three separate jobs. Then the actual network design, the VLANs, the firewall rules, the DNS setup, which turned out to be a lot more interesting than I expected going in. I didn't predict, for example, that letting two networks talk to each other on paper wouldn't be enough to make a Chromecast actually show up when I went looking for it.

What stayed constant

A few decisions held steady through the whole thing, even as everything else changed. I kept the AT&T gateway around instead of ripping it out, because it's still the box that actually connects to the internet and there was no good reason to replace something that already does that job. I put a dedicated router behind it specifically to get real control over routing, VLANs, and DNS, which the gateway alone couldn't give me. And I tried hard not to buy hardware just because it looked good in a forum thread. There's a whole page later about the gear I deliberately didn't buy, and honestly that turned out to be almost as useful a lesson as the gear I did end up buying.

What follows isn't a tutorial you can copy step by step onto your own network. It's closer to a log of decisions, a few wrong turns, and the moments where something I thought I understood turned out to be wrong once I actually tried it. If you're building something similar, I think the mistakes end up being the more useful part to read.
